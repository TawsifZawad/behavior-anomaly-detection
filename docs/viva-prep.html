01

The 60-second pitch

memorize this
Rule-based SIEM and IDS catch known attacks well, but they miss the rare, quiet ones — U2R and R2L — whose evidence looks almost like normal activity. Our framework fuses three independent viewpoints — host telemetry (Windows/Ubuntu/macOS), network alerts (Suricata), and SIEM alerts (Wazuh) — into one OS-independent pipeline. Every session is judged three ways: context-aware rules, multi-stage cross-plane correlation that only fires when two planes agree, and a lightweight Isolation Forest that learns each machine's normal across 18 behavioral features. Every finding is mapped to MITRE ATT&CK and explained, not left as a score. It catches 99.3% of real recorded attacks, scores 0.944 on NSL-KDD and 0.81 over 330k real CERT sessions — and the benchmark's own weakness on R2L/U2R is exactly why we add the host, SIEM and correlation layers on top.

03

Question & answer defense

the ones they will ask
trap

Model Performance looks almost perfect — is that real?

Those metrics come from a 234-session held-out set: 97 synthetic normal + 137 REAL EVTX attacks. The attack side is genuine recorded attacks the model never trained on — 99% caught. I openly say the normal side is synthetic; that's why we also ran CERT with 1,000 real users.

one-linerThe trustworthy figure there is 99% recall on real attacks; the normal side is synthetic and I say so.

trap

The 100-user sample demo is near-perfect — isn't that overfitting / cheating?

It's a reproducible demonstration on clean synthetic data — its job is to show the pipeline works end-to-end (Rahim CRITICAL, 96 clear). Being near-perfect is expected. I never present it as a performance claim; real performance is EVTX, NSL-KDD and CERT.

one-linerDemo = "does it work"; benchmarks = "how well". Two different questions.

honest metric

CERT accuracy is 97% — that's excellent, right?

No — on CERT, malicious days are only 1.5% of the data, so accuracy is misleading (calling everything "normal" already scores 98.5%). The honest metrics for rare positives are ROC-AUC 0.81 and per-insider detection: 89% at a 10% review budget, 97% at 20%.

one-linerAccuracy and F1 mislead on 1.5%-rare data; I report ROC-AUC and detection-at-budget instead.

technical

Why Isolation Forest and not deep learning (CNN/LSTM)?

Three reasons: it needs no labelled attacks (U2R/R2L are rare on any real machine); it's sub-linear and lightweight enough to run continuously on an ordinary endpoint; and it's built for the "few and different" profile of a rare-attack session. Lv & Ding's CNN+LSTM is heavier and needs labelled data.

one-linerLabel-free, lightweight, and matched to how rare attacks actually look.

technical

Isolation Forest is old — what's your novelty?

The contribution is architectural, not algorithmic. No prior work combines Wazuh + Suricata + Isolation Forest + MITRE + cross-platform host telemetry + cross-plane correlation in one lightweight framework aimed at U2R/R2L. The novelty is the fusion and the cross-plane correlation, not the classifier.

one-linerWe don't claim a new algorithm — we claim a new, honest way of combining established ones.

honest limit

Why is R2L only 44.9% on NSL-KDD?

At the network-flow level, most test R2L attacks are novel types that look identical to normal traffic — there's no signal to separate. It's a genuine unsupervised ceiling, not a tuning failure. This is precisely the empirical evidence that motivates adding host, SIEM and correlation layers.

one-linerThat low number is my argument for the multi-layer design — I use it, I don't hide it.

technical

What exactly is cross-plane correlation?

A correlation chain that fires only when two independent telemetry planes agree. Example: an obfuscated PowerShell command (host) plus contact with a known-malicious address (network) becomes a Confirmed C2 channel [CRITICAL] — a conclusion neither plane can reach alone. It raises confidence and cuts false positives.

one-linerTwo independent viewpoints agreeing is stronger evidence than either alone.

technical

How do you keep false positives down?

An allowlist drops the tool's own and operator-approved activity first; a two-tier risk model separates "behaving differently" (low weight) from "genuinely dangerous" (high weight); and correlation requires agreement before a CRITICAL verdict. A change of routine gets noticed, not accused.

one-linerDeviation earns a look; a dangerous action earns an alert — they're weighted differently.

technical

Why a per-user model instead of one global model?

A 2 a.m. logon is perfectly normal for a night-shift admin but a red flag for a nine-to-five worker. A single shared model can't make that distinction; a per-user baseline learns "unlike this user".

one-linerNormal is personal — the baseline has to be too.

technical

Couldn't an attacker slowly poison the baseline?

We separate learning from monitoring: a dedicated learn step establishes the baseline; watch mode monitors against the already-learned baseline instead of retraining on whatever it sees. Attack sessions are never fed to the model as normal.

one-linerThe baseline is established deliberately, not silently updated from live traffic.

scope

Does it actually run live with Wazuh and Suricata?

Yes — demonstrated on an Ubuntu VM. Suricata's live eve.json is ingested through config/sensors.local.conf; a generated network alert surfaces as a network indicator in own mode, and cross-plane chains fire when host evidence is present too. It also runs host-only if the sensors are absent.

one-linerLive-validated on a real VM; sensors are optional, not required.

scope

Why did per-day CERT detection stay modest?

Genuine insiders behave normally on most days, so per-day detection is inherently modest — but per-insider we catch 89% at a 10% review budget. We used only CERT's logon/file/USB signals; e-mail and web features (which CERT also contains) would lift it further and are named as future work.

one-linerYou only need to catch an insider once — per-insider we get 89–97%.
